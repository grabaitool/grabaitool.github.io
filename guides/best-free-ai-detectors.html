Best Free AI Detectors (2026): Honest Picks, Real Limits & How They Work

An AI detector answers one narrow question: does this text (or image) look like a model wrote it? Teachers check essays with them, editors check submissions, and students check their own drafts before handing them in. The free versions below are the ones worth using — ordered by how much you can actually do before a paywall appears.

One warning that belongs at the top, not the bottom: no detector is proof. Every tool on this page can flag human writing as AI and miss AI writing that was edited by a human. Use a score as a reason to look closer and ask questions — never as a verdict on its own.

1. GPTZero — the teacher's detector

GPTZero was built for education, and it shows: it highlights the exact sentences it finds suspicious instead of returning one opaque percentage, which makes its reports discussable in a classroom. Free scans cover a small monthly allowance; paid educator and team plans unlock higher limits.

Best for: teachers and anyone who needs to show their working, not just a number.

2. ZeroGPT — free, no sign-up

ZeroGPT is the zero-friction option: basic detection is free without an account, bundled with paraphrasing and plagiarism tools on the same site. Paid plans add batch file uploads and higher word limits. Its interface is busier than GPTZero's, but for a one-off check it is the fastest route on this page.

Best for: a quick one-off check with no account and no card.

3. QuillBot's AI detector — inside a writing suite

QuillBot is better known for paraphrasing, but its free tier is generous across the whole suite and its detector sits alongside the grammar checker and summarizer students already use. Premium (from $9.95/mo) removes the word caps. If you are checking a draft you also intend to improve, having the detector and the editing tools in one place is genuinely convenient.

Best for: students checking and polishing the same draft in one tool.

4. SynthID Detector — a different kind of check

Google's SynthID Detector does not guess from style at all: it looks for the invisible SynthID watermark embedded in AI-generated images, video and audio. It is free to use, and where a watermark exists it is far more reliable than any text classifier — but it only detects content that carries the watermark, so a negative result proves nothing. For media checks, it is the right tool; for essays, use the three above.

Best for: checking whether an image, video or audio file carries an AI watermark.

When free is not enough: the institutional options

Turnitin is the academic standard for similarity and AI-writing checks, but it is sold as an institutional licence to schools and universities — individuals cannot buy a login. If your school uses it, its check is the one that matters, and no free tool replicates it. For publishers scanning at scale, Originality.ai pairs AI detection with plagiarism and site-wide audits on a paid plan; a small trial is sometimes available.

How detectors work (and why they fail)

Text detectors mostly measure two things: perplexity — how predictable the word choices are — and burstiness — how much sentence rhythm varies. AI text tends to be smooth and predictable; human text is spikier. That is also the failure mode: formulaic human writing (legal text, non-native English, heavily edited prose) scores as "AI", and AI text that a human rewrote scores as human. Treat every percentage as a probability with wide error bars.

And a note on the mirror-image search: tools like Undetectable AI rewrite AI drafts to sound more natural, with built-in detector checks. Rewriting your own draft for clarity is ordinary editing; submitting machine text as your own unaided work is an academic-integrity question no tool can answer for you.

Our verdict

For most people: GPTZero for evidence you can discuss, ZeroGPT for a fast free check, SynthID for media. Run anything important through two detectors, read the flagged sentences yourself, and remember what the score is — a signal, not a sentence.

How to test a detector yourself before you trust it

Do not judge any detector by its marketing page. Run this ten-minute test instead. Take three pieces of text: one you wrote yourself before AI writing tools existed (an old email or essay works), one freshly generated by a chatbot with a plain prompt, and one generated text that you then edited by hand for a few minutes. Paste each into the detector and write down the scores. A useful tool separates the three clearly. A weak one flags your own pre-AI writing as machine-made or waves the edited AI text through — and both failures happen more often than the vendors admit. Repeat the test with a non-native speaker's writing if you can; detectors are notoriously harsher on simpler, more regular English, which is one reason false accusations land so unevenly on international students.

If you have been falsely flagged

It happens, and there is a sensible way to respond. First, do not panic-edit the document — that destroys your evidence. Show your process instead: drafts, notes, version history in Google Docs or Word, and earlier work in the same voice are all stronger proof than a second detector's score. Second, run the same text through two other detectors from this list; if three tools disagree wildly, that disagreement itself is the point — these scores are estimates, not measurements. Third, ask what evidence standard is being applied. A detector score alone, with no corroborating signs (no sudden style change, no factual hallucinations, no impossible turnaround), is not a fair basis for a misconduct finding, and most institutions know it. The honest summary: free detectors are useful smoke alarms. They are not courts, and nobody should treat a percentage on a web page as one.
