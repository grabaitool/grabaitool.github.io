AI Meeting Assistants Explained: How They Work and How to Choose One (2026)

What an AI meeting assistant actually does

An AI meeting assistant joins or observes your video calls, records them, and turns the conversation into searchable text: a full transcript, a summary of the decisions made, and a list of action items with owners. The category exists because meeting notes are the admin work everybody hates and nobody trusts — the human note-taker misses things, interprets generously, and becomes a bottleneck for everyone who was not in the room. A good assistant removes that job entirely and gives the whole team the same record of what was said and agreed. The six tools in this guide all do that core job; they differ in how they join the call, what they do with the transcript afterwards, and what they cost.

The first decision: a bot in the meeting, or no bot at all

Most assistants — Fireflies.ai, Otter.ai, tl;dv and MeetGeek — join the call as a visible participant, usually named something like "Fathom Notetaker" or "Fireflies.ai Notetaker". That visibility is a feature: everyone can see recording is happening, which keeps consent simple. Fathom follows this model too, but keeps its free plan unusually generous — unlimited recordings and transcripts, with Premium (from $15 per user/mo on annual billing) adding advanced AI summaries and CRM sync. The alternative design is Granola, which never joins the call at all. It sits on your own machine, enhances the rough notes you type yourself, and builds the polished summary from your notes plus the call audio it captures locally. No bot appears in anyone's participant list, which is why Granola has become the default recommendation for people whose clients or executives dislike being recorded by a robot — paid plans start at $14 per user/mo.

What happens to the transcript is where tools diverge

A transcript by itself is a chore to read, so judge these tools on the layer above it. Fireflies.ai (Pro from about $18/mo) leans into search and conversation intelligence: ask questions across your whole meeting history, track how often topics come up, and push summaries into the tools your team already uses. Otter.ai (Pro from about $17/mo) is the veteran of live transcription — its real-time captions and speaker-labelled transcripts made it the default in universities and media long before AI summaries were fashionable, and it remains the safest pick when the live transcript itself is the product. tl;dv (paid plans from $18 per user/mo on annual billing) is built for people who live in recorded calls but rarely rewatch them: it clips the moments that matter, timestamps insights, and turns hours of customer calls into short highlight reels your team will actually open. MeetGeek (paid plans from $15 per user/mo on annual billing) adds meeting analytics — talk ratios, participation, sentiment — which suits managers coaching a sales team more than individuals dodging note duty.

Consent, privacy and the recordings nobody thinks about

Every tool here records people, and that carries obligations a free trial will not warn you about. In many jurisdictions everyone on the call must know recording is happening; the joining-bot design handles this naturally, while bot-free tools put the announcement duty on you. Check where recordings are stored and how long they are kept, especially if your meetings cover clients, patients, students or anything under an NDA. Enterprise buyers should ask about retention controls and whether audio is used to train models — the reputable tools in this category let you opt out or contract out of training entirely. One practical rule covers most teams: record internal meetings freely, announce and confirm on external calls, and never record a conversation whose sensitivity you would not want summarised by a machine in the first place.

How to choose, by the meetings you actually have

Start free with Fathom if you want the core job done well at no cost — unlimited recording and transcription on a free plan is unmatched in this category, and the summaries are strong enough for most team meetings. Choose Granola if a bot joining your calls is a social or political problem, or if you already take rough notes and want them upgraded rather than replaced. Pick Fireflies.ai when the archive matters — months of searchable meetings your whole company can query. Pick Otter.ai for live events, lectures and interviews where the transcript is needed during the call, not after. Pick tl;dv or MeetGeek if you manage a sales or customer-facing team and the coaching data — clips, talk ratios, recurring objections — is worth more than the notes themselves. Whichever you choose, run it on ten real meetings before paying: accuracy on your accents, your jargon and your call quality is the only benchmark that counts, and every tool here lets you test that for free.

Accuracy: what to expect, honestly

Transcription quality is the foundation everything else stands on, and it varies more by meeting than by tool. Clean audio, one speaker at a time, standard accents: every assistant here produces transcripts you can quote. Overlapping talk, heavy accents, poor microphones and niche jargon all drag accuracy down, and the summaries inherit every error — an assistant that mishears a number will confidently summarise the wrong number. Two habits protect you. Keep names and product terms in a custom vocabulary list if your tool offers one (several here do), and treat AI action items as drafts: the thirty-second skim before the summary goes to the team is the cheapest quality control in your whole meeting culture. The tools are genuinely good; they are not court reporters.

A two-week rollout that actually sticks

Week one, run the assistant on your own internal meetings only and tell nobody to change anything — the goal is a personal verdict on accuracy with your team's voices and vocabulary. Week two, share two or three summaries in the places the team already reads (your chat channel, the project doc) instead of asking anyone to log into a new dashboard; adoption follows usefulness delivered where people already are. Only then introduce it to client or external calls, with the announcement habit agreed in advance. Teams that launch these tools with a policy memo get resistance; teams that launch them with one undeniably useful summary get requests. Let the second group be you.
